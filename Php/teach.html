For Ahmad · not for Fatima

How to teach this course

Eleven sessions, each one with the minutes, the sketches to draw, and the sentences worth saying exactly. Open the session before the call, keep it on your second screen during it.

The four moves

Every single concept, all course long, in this order. Never start at step 3, which is what most people do and why explanations stop landing.

Move 1Name it

One sentence, plain words, no code on screen. “An array is a row of numbered boxes.”

Move 2Draw it

On the whiteboard, by hand, while she watches it appear. The drawing is the explanation; the words are the caption.

Move 3Show it

Now the editor. Type it live, slowly, with the drawing still visible. Point back at the drawing as you type each part.

Move 4She does it

Her screen, her hands, you silent. If you skip this one she will nod at everything and be able to do none of it.

The one habit that matters most: before you ever press run, ask “what will this print?” and wait for an answer. The gap between her guess and the real output is the entire lesson. Running it first throws that away.

Running the board on Zoom

Zoom’s own whiteboard, not a PowerPoint you flick through. The drawing appearing in real time is what holds attention; a finished slide does not.

Share → WhiteboardYour default for anything conceptual. Sessions 0A, and the first ten minutes of 5, 6 and 8.
Share → Screen → AnnotateShare the slide or the code, then press Annotate in the toolbar to draw straight over it. This is how you circle a line of code mid-explanation.
Pen, redNew idea being introduced.
Pen, blueHer answer, her guess, her words. Keeping her ideas in a different colour is a small thing that makes her feel like a participant.
Eraser, not ClearRub out one wrong bit and leave the rest. Clear → All Drawings wipes everything — only at a real topic change.
Undo (Ctrl+Z)Works on the whiteboard. Use it instead of scribbling over a mistake.
SaveThe whiteboard toolbar has a Save button — it writes a PNG. Press it at the end of every session and send it to her. Her notes, in your handwriting, free.
Give her the penOnce per session, minimum. Share → Annotate works for her too — make her draw the flowchart while you watch. Nothing exposes a gap faster.
Stop Share to talkWhen something goes wrong emotionally, come off the screen share and be two faces talking. Do not debug frustration through a shared editor.

When to wipe the board

Erase the working, keep the conclusion. At the end of each block you should be left with one box, one definition or one rule still on screen — the thing you want her to remember. Wipe the rest. A board that still has everything on it at minute 50 teaches nothing, because she cannot tell which part mattered.

The shape of every call

Warm-up (she explains one line of last week’s work out loud) → you explain the new idea with the four moves → she drives for twenty minutes while you stay quiet → one arcade round → homework. Same order every week. Predictability is not boring here; it is what lets her stop worrying about the format and think about the content.
